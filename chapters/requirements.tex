\subsection{Problem Context and Origin}
\label{subsec:problem-context}

The project originated with Spectrum Software \& Consulting Pvt. Ltd. (SSCL), a software development organisation. Engineering leadership at
SSCL monitored the health of their software projects through four to five
separate tool dashboards such as a version control platform (e.g., Github), an issue-tracking and sprint-planning platform (e.g., Jira), a static code-analysis service (e.g., SonarQube), and a CI/CD service (e.g., Jenkins). Each dashboard presented its own metrics in its
own vocabulary, with no shared notion of whether a project was, overall, in good
or poor health. Assessing the state of a single project required visiting every
dashboard in turn and mentally reconciling the results; assessing a portfolio of
projects this way was impractical.

SSCL proposed a platform that would ingest data from these separate tools,
translate heterogeneous raw metrics into a common, comparable scale, and present
the result in a single view. The system described in this report addresses that
problem. It is explicitly positioned as a trend indicator system rather
than a real-time monitoring tool. Its purpose is to reveal how projects and teams
are trending over weeks and months, not to report the state of a build in the
last five minutes.

\subsection{Requirements Elicitation}
\label{subsec:elicitation}

Requirements were gathered through three complementary channels. The primary
channel was a series of meetings with the Chief Technical Officer of
SSCL, who originated the concept and articulated the operational pain point,
the tools in use, and the decisions the system was intended to support. A
secondary channel was an intern at SSCL with prior exposure to the concept,
who provided practical grounding on how the organisation's teams actually work
day to day. The third channel was the team's own analysis such as study of the public
APIs exposed by the candidate tools to establish which metrics were obtainable in
practice, and derivation of a scoring model from that feasible metric set.
Throughout, the university supervisor provided guidance on scope, methodology and
academic rigour.

\subsection{Scope and Approach}
\label{subsec:standards-approach}

This section identifies the legal obligations, engineering standards and codes of
practice that govern the system, states why each applies, and records how the
design responds to it. Three categories are distinguished, because they bind the
project with differing force. Contractual obligations arising from the agreement
with SSCL are binding without qualification. Statutory data-protection
obligations are treated as a reference model rather than a directly binding
regime, for the reasons given in Section~\ref{subsec:data-protection}. Engineering
standards are adopted voluntarily as recognised good practice, and conformance to
them is claimed only where it can be evidenced.

Where a standard is only partially satisfied, this is stated explicitly rather
than omitted. Section~\ref{subsec:compliance-summary} consolidates the identified
gaps, each of which is either mitigated, accepted with justification, or carried
forward as work required before the system is handed over for production use.

\subsection{Data Protection and Privacy}
\label{subsec:data-protection}

\paragraph{Why it applies.}
The system processes data derived from identifiable individuals. Version-control
metrics attribute commits, reviews and pull requests to named contributors;
issue-tracker metrics reference assignees, and the survey subsystem collects
free-text opinions authored by SSCL's employees. For data protection and privacy, we followed GDPR principles from the start, since building to the stricter rule was safer than redesigning the system later.

\paragraph{How the design complies.}
Five GDPR principles were treated as design constraints. 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data minimisation:} It governs what leaves the system. The prompts submitted to the external language
model are constructed so as to exclude personal names and work-item identifiers,
carrying only aggregate scores and depersonalised descriptions of operational
conditions. 
  \item \textbf{Purpose limitation:} It is reflected in the metric set, which was
restricted to indicators of project and process health rather than measures of
individual productivity. 
  \item \textbf{Storage limitation and integrity:} These are served by
encrypting credential material at rest and restricting its retrieval
(NFR-01, NFR-04). 
  \item \textbf{Anonymisation:} It is enforced structurally rather than by
policy. The survey response record carries a randomly generated submission
identifier and holds no foreign key to any user, so responses cannot be
re-associated with respondents even by an administrator with full database
access. 
  \item \textbf{Lawfulness and transparency:} In respect of automated
processing, these were addressed by disclosing to SSCL that project health context

\subsection{Partner Policy and Contractual Obligations}
\label{subsec:partner-policy}

\paragraph{Why it applies.}
Work was conducted under a non-disclosure agreement with SSCL. The agreement
governs the handling of their source-repository data, issue-tracker contents,
access credentials and internal process information, and constrains what may be
disclosed in this report.

\paragraph{How the design complies.}
Access credentials supplied by SSCL are encrypted at rest, masked in all
API responses by default, retrievable only through an explicit
administrator-restricted action, and excluded from application logs
(NFR-01, NFR-04). Where a credential is used as a grouping key during scheduled
synchronisation, it is first reduced to a one-way hash so that the operational
logs never contain the credential itself. The system integrates strictly
read-only, requesting only the API scopes necessary for metric retrieval, and
holds no capability to modify the partner's repositories, pipelines or issue
tracker (C-07). In this report, identifying details of SSCL are handled
according to the disclosure terms of the agreement.

\subsection{Third-Party Terms of Service as Codes of Practice}
\label{subsec:third-party-terms}

\paragraph{Why it applies.}
The system's core function depends on six external services, each imposing
contractual usage terms, authentication requirements and quota limits. Because
all are consumed under free service tiers (C-03), their terms constrain the
design directly rather than being a matter of billing.

\paragraph{How the design complies.}
Rate limits published by the version-control provider are respected through a
shared throttled API client that observes the provider's own rate-limit response
headers, applies bounded concurrency, and abandons a request rather than blocking
a worker indefinitely when a limit reset would require an extended wait
(NFR-15, NFR-16). Scheduled synchronisation groups projects that share an access
credential and staggers them in time, so that a single credential's quota is not
exhausted by a burst of concurrent runs.

The terms governing the language-model service warrant specific note. The service
is consumed under its free tier, whose terms permit submitted inputs to be used
by the provider for product improvement. The project responded in three ways:
the content transmitted is constrained by the data-minimisation measures of
Section~\ref{subsec:data-protection}; the limitation was documented in the
project's own technical reference as an explicit operating caution; and
SSCL was informed that the free tier is used for question generation and
response analysis, so that the decision to proceed was made by the data
controller rather than assumed by the development team.

\paragraph{Identified gap.}
Free-text survey responses are authored by developers and cannot be validated
against the data-minimisation rules before transmission; a respondent may
volunteer identifying information within their own answer. This residual risk is
recorded as G-03.

\subsection{Accessibility: WCAG 2.1 Level AA}
\label{subsec:wcag}

\paragraph{Why it applies.}
The client application is the sole interface through which managers perform
routine assessment (NFR-18). As a web interface intended for daily professional
use, it falls within the scope of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines,
against which Level~AA is the conventional target for non-public professional
software.

\paragraph{How the design complies.}
Interactive elements that are not natively focusable carry explicit roles, are
included in the tab order, and respond to keyboard activation as well as pointer
input, satisfying the keyboard-operability criteria. State-bearing controls expose
their state programmatically through the corresponding ARIA attributes.
Non-text status is never conveyed by colour alone, every health score is rendered
as a numeric value alongside its colour coding, so that the information survives
both monochrome rendering and colour-vision deficiency. Page structure uses
semantic headings, with a visually hidden heading provided where the visual design
does not call for one.

\paragraph{Identified gap.}
Conformance has not been formally audited, whether by automated tooling or by
assistive-technology testing; contrast ratios have not been measured against the
Level~AA threshold; and the charting library used for trend visualisation offers
limited screen-reader semantics. Conformance is therefore claimed as partial and
unverified, and is recorded as G-06.

\subsection{Comparative Evaluation}
\label{subsec:primary-evaluation}

\paragraph{EC-1 : Single consolidated view.} 
Solution 1 and Solution 2 both satisfy this. Each produces one store of normalised data from
which a unified view can be rendered. Solution 3 does not. A BI product can place panels
from several sources on one screen, but it preserves each tool's own metric
vocabulary; the manager still reads four sets of numbers rather than one
comparable score. This is precisely the condition SSCL asked to be relieved
of, so Solution 3 fails the originating requirement even though it appears superficially
to address it.

\paragraph{EC-2 : No modification of tool infrastructure.}
Solution 2 satisfies this completely. It requires only a read-scoped access token. Solution 1
does not. Webhooks must be registered on every repository, CI pipelines must be
altered to report outcomes, and the issue tracker must be configured to emit
events, all of which require administrative changes inside systems the team
has no authority over. Solution 3 partially satisfies it as exports can often be arranged without
structural change, though scheduled export jobs still require somewhere to run.

\paragraph{EC-3 : Free service tiers.}
Solution 2 satisfies this. Polling consumes API quota but remains within free limits when
projects sharing a credential are staggered. Solution 1 is in principle the most
economical, since events arrive without polling, but it requires an
always-available public endpoint to receive them, which is difficult to
guarantee on free hosting that suspends idle instances, and a webhook delivery
missed during suspension is lost permanently. Solution 3 fails as the BI products capable
of the required multi-source integration are commercially licensed, and the
free variants impose limits that the intended usage would exceed.

\paragraph{EC-4 : Explainable scores.}
Solution 1 and Solution 2 are equivalent here, since explainability is a property of the scoring
model rather than of how data arrives; both retain the inputs from which a score
was derived. Solution 3 partially satisfies it. Derived calculations inside a BI product
are expressible, but the tooling is oriented toward displaying values rather than
accounting for how a composite figure was assembled, and reconstructing a
per-metric contribution breakdown across heterogeneous sources would be awkward.

\paragraph{EC-5 : Extensibility.}
Solution 2 satisfies this. A new provider is a new connector behind an existing
interface, with no change to scoring. Solution 1 partially satisfies it, since each new
provider brings its own event schema, delivery semantics and registration
procedure, and not every tool emits events for the quantities of interest. Solution 3
partially satisfies it, bounded by which sources the chosen BI product supports.

\paragraph{EC-6 : Confidentiality and anonymity.}
Solution 2 satisfies this. Data is pulled into infrastructure the team controls, using
narrowly scoped read-only credentials, and anonymity is enforced in a schema the
team designed. Solution 1 partially satisfies it. The receiving endpoint is publicly
reachable by necessity and becomes an additional attack surface carrying tool
data. Solution 3 does not satisfy it adequately as routing tool data through a
third-party analytics product introduces a processor whose terms the team does
not control, which sits poorly with the non-disclosure agreement.

\paragraph{EC-7 : Tolerance of third-party failure.}
Solution 2 satisfies this. A pull that fails can simply be retried, and the previous
snapshot remains valid in the meantime; an unavailable tool degrades one
dimension of one snapshot rather than losing data. Solution 1 does not since a push
architecture loses events that arrive while the receiver is down unless the
provider offers replay, and reconstructing the gap afterwards generally requires
a pull mechanism anyway meaning Solution 1 in practice tends toward implementing Solution 2
as well. Solution 3 partially satisfies it, depending on the export mechanism.

\paragraph{EC-8 : Data currency.}
Here the ordering reverses. Solution 1 satisfies this best, delivering events within
seconds. Solution 2 only partially satisfies it because between scheduled runs the data is
stale, bounded by the sync interval. Solution 3 partially satisfies it, depending on
export frequency.

\subsection{Selection and Justification}
\label{subsec:primary-selection}

Solution 2 is selected.

Solution 3 is eliminated first, and on the strongest possible ground that it fails EC-1. It
would leave SSCL reading each tool's metrics in that tool's own terms,
which is the problem the project exists to solve. Its advantages in
implementation effort are therefore irrelevant, and its licensing cost (EC-3)
and third-party processing of tool data (EC-6) would disqualify it regardless.

Solution 1 is eliminated on feasibility rather than on merit. As an architecture it is
sound, and it is the only option that fully satisfies EC-8. But it requires
administrative changes inside the tool's systems (EC-2) that the project has
no standing to make, an always-available public endpoint that free hosting cannot
guarantee (EC-3). Its weakness on EC-7 compounds these: because missed events
cannot be recovered without a pull mechanism, a production-grade Solutoin 1 would
generally have to implement Solution 2 as well, making it a superset of the selected
solution rather than an alternative to it.

Solution 2's weakness is real and should be stated plainly. It is the weakest of the
three on data currency (EC-8). Between scheduled runs the dashboard shows the
state as of the last synchronisation, not the present moment. This was judged
acceptable, for two reasons. First, it is consistent with how the product is
positioned: as established in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-context}, the system is
a trend indicator intended to reveal trends over weeks and months, and the
decisions it supports, such as whether a project is drifting, whether a team is under
strain, do not require minute-by-minute currency. Second, the limitation is
mitigated rather than merely tolerated because an on-demand synchronisation is available
whenever a current reading is needed, so the scheduled cadence sets a floor on
freshness rather than a ceiling.

Weighed as a whole, Solution 2 satisfies every criterion that the project's constraints
make non-negotiable, and is weakest only on the one criterion the product's
positioning renders least critical.

\subsection{Candidate Approaches: Health Score Computation}
\label{subsec:secondary-candidates}

The second decision concerns how the collected metrics become the seven health
scores. Three approaches were considered.

\textbf{Approach 1 : Rule-based weighted model.} Each metric is normalised to a common
0--100 scale by an explicit formula, assigned a weight reflecting its assessed
importance, and combined into a weighted average per dimension.

\textbf{Approach 2 : Supervised machine learning.} A model is trained on historical
project data labelled with known outcomes such as projects that failed, slipped, or
succeeded, and learns to predict health from the metric vector.

\textbf{Approach 3 : Language-model judgement.} The raw metrics are presented to a large
language model, which returns an assessed score per dimension with a narrative
rationale.

\subsection{Comparative Evaluation and Selection}
\label{subsec:secondary-selection}

Approach 2 is eliminated on data availability. Supervised learning requires labelled
training data, and none exists. There is no corpus of projects annotated with
health outcomes, the industry partner has no such historical record, and C-06 establishes
that the system cannot backfill history from the source tools. A model could only
begin learning after the platform had itself accumulated years of observations,
and even then the labels, what counts as a project having gone badly, would
have to be supplied by the very managers the system is meant to inform. The
approach is circular at the outset and cold-started indefinitely.

Approach 3 is eliminated on explainability and determinism. It fails EC-5 directly because a
language model can produce a plausible narrative for a score, but that narrative
is a post-hoc rationalisation rather than an account of a computation, and it
cannot be audited against the underlying arithmetic because there is none. Two
identical inputs may yield different scores, making trend lines, the system's
central output, unreliable. It also conflicts with EC-3, since every score for
every project on every scheduled run would incur an inference cost, and with
EC-6, since it would require transmitting the full metric set for the partner's
projects to a third-party service on every run.

Approach 1 is selected. It satisfies EC-5 completely because a score is a weighted sum of
normalised inputs, so the contribution of each metric is recoverable by
construction, which is what makes the per-metric breakdown of FR-26 possible at
all. It is deterministic, so identical inputs always yield identical scores and
trend lines mean what they appear to mean. It requires no training data and so
produces meaningful output from a project's very first synchronisation. It incurs
no marginal cost per evaluation, satisfying EC-3. And because the weighting rules
are declared explicitly rather than learned, they can be documented and unit
tested, satisfying NFR-22.

The principal weakness of T1 is its weights and
thresholds are assigned by judgement rather than derived from evidence, and are
documented in this project as provisional values requiring calibration against
real project data. This is a genuine limitation, but it is a limitation of
calibration rather than of approach. The weights can be revised as
evidence accumulates without altering the structure of the model, whereas the
deficiencies identified in Approach 2 and Approahc 3 are intrinsic.

The selected approach was itself refined during development. An initial
six-category scoring model was replaced, following the team's own research, by
the seven-category model described in this report, after analysis of the
available metrics showed that the original categories conflated concerns the
collected data could distinguish and, in places, claimed distinctions the
data could not support. That the model could be restructured in this way, with
the change confined to the declared rules while the surrounding pipeline was
unaffected, is itself evidence for the maintainability argument advanced above.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{subsec:formulation-conclusion}

The system is therefore built as a scheduled-pull platform (Solution 2) with a rule-based
weighted scoring model (Approach 1). Together these satisfy every criterion that the
project's constraints render non-negotiable, and are weakest only in areas such as
data currency, and the calibration of scoring weights which are either
mitigated by design or improvable without structural change. The detailed design
of the selected solution is presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:design}.
